- **"Try the same topic again" with before and after.** _(2026-11-19)_ On a Test your speaking result, a button "Try this topic again"; the new result then shows the earlier attempt's scores beside the new ones (for example "Fluency 55 → 68") and a line of praise for what went up. Kept recordings of the same topic are grouped in "My recordings". Seen at: Cambridge's Speak & Improve keeps every recording, shows results per task, encourages recording a task again and comparing, and gives awards for checking and improving rather than for a high score (https://help.writeandimprove.com/en/articles/9652123-how-can-speak-improve-help-me-improve-my-english-speaking); Busuu gives two more attempts after feedback (https://help.busuu.com/hc/en-gb/articles/19367617005970-Mastering-language-skills-through-speaking-practice). Why: feedback is only useful if the student acts on it straight away, and today the result page sends him to another topic; improving on his own earlier attempt is a fair goal for a weak speaker, with no comparison to anyone else. Size **S** (the last twelve recordings and their scores are already on the device). Nothing new that is paid; a second attempt uses one more AI feedback request inside the existing daily limit. No accounts, no decision needed.
- **Words to review today.** _(2026-11-18)_ Every lesson phrase a student has practised gets a "next review" date on the device, with growing gaps: the next day, then 3, 7, 14 and 30 days; a correct answer moves it one step on, a wrong one sends it back to the first step. The home "today" card shows "8 words to review" and opens a five-minute round built from what the site already has (hear the phrase and choose its picture, see the picture and choose the words). Seen at: Memrise describes fixed steps from hours to months, with a wrong answer returning the item to the first step (https://www.memrise.com/blog/february-product-roundup); Duolingo explains why spaced reviews beat repeating a word many times in one evening, and chooses review items by time and accuracy (https://blog.duolingo.com/spaced-repetition-for-learning); Lingvist times each repeat for just before a word would be forgotten (https://lingvist.com/blog/spaced-repetition-in-learning/). Why: a finished lesson is never seen again here, so its words fade within a week or two; a small daily review is also a natural "one thing to do today" for the streak. Size **M**. Nothing paid, no accounts, no decision needed. Keep the round short and capped (for example 10 items) so a student back after a holiday is not met by a pile of 80.
- **Practice days on the teacher's Students page.** _(2026-11-17)_ Add "streak" and "days practised this week" to the progress summary each student already sends, and show them as a column next to "last practised". Seen at: Languagenut presents streaks as a sign of effort for teachers (https://help.languagenut.com/article/343-student-streaks); Khan Academy encourages teachers to run streak challenges in class (https://blog.khanacademy.org/keeping-your-streak-alive-insights-tips-from-the-last-6-months/). Why: the teacher can praise steady effort, not only high scores — a weak student who practises four days a week deserves to be noticed. Size **S–M** (two more numbers inside the saved summary; no database change expected). Nothing paid; no accounts. Note: like the rest of the summary, the numbers come from the student's device and are not verified.
- **My words.** _(2026-11-18)_ A page that lists every word and phrase from the lessons a student has finished, grouped by level, each with its picture and a 🔊 button, and a small mark the site sets by itself: new, learning, known (from how the student did in Match, Speak and the review rounds). A counter at the top: "You know 23 words". Seen at: Busuu's vocabulary trainer, which saves the words met in lessons automatically and shows which are weak (https://www.busuu.com/en/languages/spaced-repetition); Cambridge's free A1 activities include a picture dictionary with pronunciation practice (https://assets.cambridgeenglish.org/portal/learner/a1/vocabulary.html); the British Council's vocabulary pages teach meaning, pronunciation and spelling together (https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/vocabulary). Why: percentages on My progress say little to a 13-year-old; "23 words I know" is something he can see grow and show a parent, and the page is a quick place to revise before a school test. Size **S** for the list with pictures and sound, **M** with the marks. Nothing paid, no accounts, no decision needed. Works best after "Practise my mistakes" or "Words to review today" exist, since they supply the marks.
- **Sound pairs: "Which word did you hear?"** _(2026-11-19)_ A new small part of Pronounce built on the sounds Arabic speakers mix up most: p / b, f / v, ch / sh, the vowels in "sit" and "seat", and words that start with two or three consonants. Each set is a handful of word pairs that differ in one sound (written by us). Step 1, ears: the site says one word of a pair and the student taps the one he heard, ten quick questions. Step 2, mouth: the student says both words of a pair and each is checked the way the Pronounce test already checks a word. Seen at: Duolingo lists sound drills and pairs of near-identical words among its speaking exercises (https://blog.duolingo.com/covering-all-the-bases-duolingos-approach-to-speaking-skills); ELSA has "which of two similar words did you hear" checks, as described in a review (https://www.fluentu.com/blog/reviews/elsa-speak/); two pronunciation teachers' guides for Arabic speakers name the same few sounds (https://www.boldvoice.com/blog/english-pronunciation-arabic-speakers, https://hadarshemesh.com/magazine/english-for-arabic-speakers/); a plain method for practising such pairs (https://www.leonardoenglish.com/blog/minimal-pairs). Why: Pronounce today is organised by letter and spelling for every learner alike; nothing targets the exact sounds a Saudi student's first language does not have, and a student who cannot hear the difference between "park" and "bark" cannot fix it by repeating. The listening step also works with no microphone and in every browser. Size **S** for the listening step alone, **M** with the speaking step. Nothing new that is paid (the speaking step uses the Pronounce check that is already there, inside its daily limit); no accounts. **Needs the owner's ear once**: the listening step uses the device's computer voice, and the agents cannot hear whether it says "pin" and "bin" clearly enough — he or a teacher should listen to each pair before students see it.
- **Hear yourself next to the model.** _(2026-11-19)_ (2026-11-19: the small version — the Pronounce test where the full pronunciation check is on — is in "Next"; what is left of this idea is the basic check and lesson Speak, size M, to be tried on real phones.) After a student records a word in the Pronounce test (and later a phrase in lesson Speak), a "▶ Me" button appears beside "🔊 Listen", so he can play the model, then his own voice, then try again. The recording stays in the page's memory for that word only: not saved, not sent anywhere new. Seen at: Busuu's speaking practice lets a learner play his own recording and the fluent speaker's before another try (https://help.busuu.com/hc/en-gb/articles/19367617005970-Mastering-language-skills-through-speaking-practice); Cambridge's Speak & Improve advises learners to listen back to their recordings to find the unclear parts and compare after a retry (https://help.writeandimprove.com/en/articles/9652123-how-can-speak-improve-help-me-improve-my-english-speaking); Duolingo's advice for shy learners includes recording yourself (https://blog.duolingo.com/introvert-language-learning-tips/). Why: a score of 62% does not tell a student what was wrong; hearing his own "bark" straight after the model's "park" often does, and it costs no extra call to the speech service. Test your speaking and the voice test already play recordings back; the two places where single words and phrases are drilled do not. Size **S** where the full pronunciation check is on (the page already holds the recording it sends); **M** to cover the basic check and lesson Speak as well, because the browser's speech recognition and a second recorder do not share the microphone reliably on every phone — that part must be tried on real phones. Nothing paid, no accounts, no decision needed.
- **Practise my mistakes.** _(2026-11-18)_ (The first version — Match game and lesson Speak — was built on 2026-11-18; what is left of this idea is the listening exercises.) The site remembers, on the device, the items a student got wrong (a wrong pair in a lesson's Match game, a phrase not said clearly in Speak, a wrong answer in a listening exercise) and offers a short round of up to ten of them from the home page and My progress: "Practise my mistakes (7)". An item leaves the list after it is answered correctly twice. Seen at: Duolingo's Practice tab, whose "Mistakes" session replays a learner's earlier errors (https://blog.duolingo.com/guide-to-duolingo-practice-hub/); Busuu repeats the words a learner keeps forgetting until they are recalled (https://www.busuu.com/en/languages/spaced-repetition). Why: today a wrong answer is only counted and then forgotten by the site; the words a student gets wrong are exactly the ones worth ten more minutes, and a list that gets shorter is a clear, reachable goal. Size **M** (S if the first version covers only the Match game and lesson Speak). Nothing paid, no accounts, no decision needed. The list lives on the device like the rest of a student's progress.
- **Show which sound was weak, with one tip.** _(2026-11-19)_ When the full pronunciation check scores a word in the Pronounce test, show the word's sounds as small coloured boxes (clear / nearly / unclear) instead of only one number, and under the weakest one a single line written by us on how to make that sound (for example for p: lips together, then a small puff of air — hold a paper in front of your mouth and make it move). Seen at: ELSA marks each sound of a word in green, orange or red and explains how to make a sound that was wrong (review: https://www.fluentu.com/blog/reviews/elsa-speak/; a competitor's comparison describing the colours: https://www.talkdrill.com/blog/compare/elsa-speak-vs-speechling/); Busuu marks unclear words in red and adds advice (https://help.busuu.com/hc/en-gb/articles/19367617005970-Mastering-language-skills-through-speaking-practice). Why: "say it again" without knowing which part was wrong is guessing; a 13-year-old who sees that only the first sound of "park" is red knows what to change. Size **M** (the server asks the speech service for scores per sound as well as per word, and about a dozen tips are written for the sounds in today's idea 1). Uses the **paid speech service already switched on** — the same number of calls, no new service or key; no accounts. **Needs the owner**: agents cannot speak, so whether the per-sound scores are fair for a real student's voice can only be judged by him or a teacher on the live site before it is shown to students; whether the service returns per-sound scores for British English should be confirmed in its documentation by the developer first.
- **Words of the week from the teacher.** _(2026-11-18)_ In teacher mode, a teacher ticks up to ten phrases from the lessons as this week's words; they travel in the course file students already load, and each student's home page offers "This week's words: practise 3 today" until all ten have been answered correctly. Seen at: Cambridge English's advice to families to keep a box of the week's new words and take a few out each day to go over again (https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/learning-english/parents-and-children/your-childs-interests/practising-vocabulary-outside-the-classroom/); the British Council's children's site organises word practice as small sets on one subject (https://learnenglishkids.britishcouncil.org/grammar-vocabulary/word-games). Why: it ties the site to what the class is doing in school this week, which is the strongest reason a student has to open it, and it is a few words a day rather than a whole lesson. Size **M**. Nothing paid, no accounts. **Needs the owner's decision**: it only works if teachers really publish a course file (`course.json`) each week — otherwise the weekly words would need to be stored on the class server, which is a bigger change.
- **Class goal: everyone's practice adds up.** _(2026-11-17)_ The teacher sets one target for the class (for example 200 finished activities this month) and optionally writes the reward; each student sees a bar "Our class: 124 / 200" that every student's practice fills, and a celebration when it is reached. Seen at: Khan Academy's class challenges where each student's points go to a shared class goal and the teacher chooses the reward (https://blog.khanacademy.org/five-ways-khan-academy-is-making-student-practice-more-motivating/); Duolingo's shared quests between friends (https://blog.duolingo.com/friends-quests/); Pearson recommends team goals over individual ranking (https://www.pearson.com/languages/en-ia/community/blogs/gamification-techniques-teaching-3-26.html). Why: it is motivation without a ranking table — nobody is shown at the bottom, and weaker students help the class as much as strong ones. Size **M** (the server must store the goal and return a class total to students; probably one small new table). Nothing paid, no student accounts. **Needs the owner's decision**: is a class total visible to students acceptable, and who chooses the reward.
