- **"Try the same topic again" with before and after.** _(2026-11-19)_ On a Test your speaking result, a button "Try this topic again"; the new result then shows the earlier attempt's scores beside the new ones (for example "Fluency 55 → 68") and a line of praise for what went up. Kept recordings of the same topic are grouped in "My recordings". Seen at: Cambridge's Speak & Improve keeps every recording, shows results per task, encourages recording a task again and comparing, and gives awards for checking and improving rather than for a high score (https://help.writeandimprove.com/en/articles/9652123-how-can-speak-improve-help-me-improve-my-english-speaking); Busuu gives two more attempts after feedback (https://help.busuu.com/hc/en-gb/articles/19367617005970-Mastering-language-skills-through-speaking-practice). Why: feedback is only useful if the student acts on it straight away, and today the result page sends him to another topic; improving on his own earlier attempt is a fair goal for a weak speaker, with no comparison to anyone else. Size **S** (the last twelve recordings and their scores are already on the device). Nothing new that is paid; a second attempt uses one more AI feedback request inside the existing daily limit. No accounts, no decision needed.
- **Words to review today.** _(2026-11-18)_ Every lesson phrase a student has practised gets a "next review" date on the device, with growing gaps: the next day, then 3, 7, 14 and 30 days; a correct answer moves it one step on, a wrong one sends it back to the first step. The home "today" card shows "8 words to review" and opens a five-minute round built from what the site already has (hear the phrase and choose its picture, see the picture and choose the words). Seen at: Memrise describes fixed steps from hours to months, with a wrong answer returning the item to the first step (https://www.memrise.com/blog/february-product-roundup); Duolingo explains why spaced reviews beat repeating a word many times in one evening, and chooses review items by time and accuracy (https://blog.duolingo.com/spaced-repetition-for-learning); Lingvist times each repeat for just before a word would be forgotten (https://lingvist.com/blog/spaced-repetition-in-learning/). Why: a finished lesson is never seen again here, so its words fade within a week or two; a small daily review is also a natural "one thing to do today" for the streak. Size **M**. Nothing paid, no accounts, no decision needed. Keep the round short and capped (for example 10 items) so a student back after a holiday is not met by a pile of 80.
- **Listening for teenagers: a short conversation with questions.** _(2026-11-20)_ A new kind of listening exercise for the A2 and B1 groups: (1) before listening, match four or five key words with their meanings or pictures; (2) listen to a short conversation between two of the voices the site already has (about one minute, written by us: at school, planning a weekend, a phone call, a shop); (3) answer five questions — true / false and choose one of three; (4) after checking, the script opens with the line that holds each answer marked. Seen at: the British Council's listening lessons, one page per recording by level A1–C1, with a preparation task before the audio, two tasks after it, the transcript and a worksheet (https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/listening/b1/interview-about-listening-skills); Randall's ESL Cyber Listening Lab — easy / intermediate / difficult, a before-listening step, multiple-choice questions with an answer at once, then the script (https://esl-lab.com/, handout: https://www.esl-lab.com/wp-content/uploads/handouts/esl-lab.pdf); ELLLO — recording, script, a few words to learn and a quiz in each lesson (https://www.elllo.org/home/index.htm); Duolingo's radio-style lessons with a true / false check on what was said (https://blog.duolingo.com/duoradio-listening-practice). Why: all 84 listening exercises here are in one group ("A1 listening") and are the young-learner kind — colour, move, tick, draw lines. A 15-year-old at A2 or B1 has nothing to listen to at his level, and school listening tests are exactly this: a conversation and questions. Size **M** (a new exercise type with its teacher editor, plus the first six or so conversations; more can then be added by the content agent). Nothing paid, no accounts. **Needs the owner's ear once**: the conversations would be read by the device's computer voices, as today; he or a teacher should say whether a one-minute conversation in those voices is pleasant enough, or whether idea "A teacher's own voice" below should come first.

A counter at the top: "You know 23 words". - **Sound pairs: "Which word did you hear?"** _(2026-11-19)_ A new small part of Pronounce built on the sounds Arabic speakers mix up most: p / b, f / v, ch / sh, the vowels in "sit" and "seat", and words that start with two or three consonants. Each set is a handful of word pairs that differ in one sound (written by us). Step 1, ears: the site says one word of a pair and the student taps the one he heard, ten quick questions. Step 2, mouth: the student says both words of a pair and each is checked the way the Pronounce test already checks a word. Seen at: Duolingo lists sound drills and pairs of near-identical words among its speaking exercises (https://blog.duolingo.com/covering-all-the-bases-duolingos-approach-to-speaking-skills); ELSA has "which of two similar words did you hear" checks, as described in a review (https://www.fluentu.com/blog/reviews/elsa-speak/); two pronunciation teachers' guides for Arabic speakers name the same few sounds (https://www.boldvoice.com/blog/english-pronunciation-arabic-speakers, https://hadarshemesh.com/magazine/english-for-arabic-speakers/); a plain method for practising such pairs (https://www.leonardoenglish.com/blog/minimal-pairs). Why: Pronounce today is organised by letter and spelling for every learner alike; nothing targets the exact sounds a Saudi student's first language does not have, and a student who cannot hear the difference between "park" and "bark" cannot fix it by repeating. The listening step also works with no microphone and in every browser. Size **S** for the listening step alone, **M** with the speaking step. Nothing new that is paid (the speaking step uses the Pronounce check that is already there, inside its daily limit); no accounts. **Needs the owner's ear once**: the listening step uses the device's computer voice, and the agents cannot hear whether it says "pin" and "bin" clearly enough — he or a teacher should listen to each pair before students see it.
- **Listen and build the sentence.** _(2026-11-20)_ (2026-11-20: the tap-the-words version is in "Next"; what is left of this idea is the typing version for B1–B2, size M.) A short round made from the phrases of lessons the student has finished: he hears a phrase (with "Slowly" and as many replays as he wants) and no text. At A1–A2 he taps the words into order from a row of word buttons that includes two extra words; at B1–B2 he types the sentence. The check is word by word — right, missing, different — capitals, full stops and apostrophes do not matter and "8" counts as "eight"; "Show the answer" counts the phrase as not known. Seen at: 7ESL's dictation, one line at a time with a word-by-word check that ignores punctuation and capitals (https://7esl.com/listening/dictation/); Speechling, which marks the right and wrong words and brings back an item the learner gave up on (https://speechling.com/help/dictation); DailyDictation's four steps — listen, type, check, say it aloud (https://dailydictation.com); EnglishClub's dictations by length, from one line to a paragraph (https://englishclub.com/listening/dictation.htm); a Cambridge paper for teachers recommends five minutes of writing down short stretches of speech, because the usual trouble is not recognising words the learner already knows (https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/Images/524275-second-language-listening-where-are-we-john-field-cambridge-english-teacher-.pdf). Why: in a lesson the student always sees the words while he hears them, so he never has to catch a sentence by ear alone; this is the one exercise that shows him exactly which word he did not catch. It reuses phrases that are already on the device. Size **S** for the tap-the-words version, **M** with typing. Nothing paid, no accounts, no decision needed. A wrong phrase can go to "Practise my mistakes"; a finished round should count for the streak.
- **Little words: "What did you hear?"** _(2026-11-20)_ Ten quick questions on the small words that disappear in normal speech. The student hears a short sentence at normal speed and either chooses which of two sentences was said ("He's late" / "He was late", "I can swim" / "I can't swim", "I'll go" / "I go") or taps how many words it had ("What do you want?" = 4); then the sentence appears and is played once more slowly. Sentences written by us from words in the A1–A2 lessons. Seen at: Duolingo's "listen for the missing word" exercises, first with text and later with sound only, and its slow-play button (https://blog.duolingo.com/covering-all-the-bases-duolingos-approach-to-listening-skills/); three articles for teachers that describe short dictations of everyday chunks, "how many words?" counting and same / different listening, because learners fail to recognise known words when they run together (https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/Images/524275-second-language-listening-where-are-we-john-field-cambridge-english-teacher-.pdf, https://ihworld.com/ih-journal/issues/issue-50/developing-word-recognition-sub-skills-in-listening, https://teachingenglishwithoxford.oup.com/2012/05/17/decoding-skills-listening-comprehension/). Why: a student can know "do", "you" and "want" and still not recognise them said together; nothing on the site practises this, and it is what makes real speech (and the school listening test) feel "too fast". Size **S**. Nothing paid, no accounts. **Needs the owner's ear once**: it only works if the device's computer voice really says "can" and "can't", "he's" and "he was" differently at normal speed — the agents cannot hear that; he or a teacher should listen to each pair on a phone before students see it, and pairs that are not clear should be dropped.
